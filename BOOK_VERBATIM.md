## Range Contraction and Expansion

The stock's range refers to the difference between the highest and lowest trading prices over a specific period. Range

---

## PAGE 27 — printed page 19

contraction occurs when prices remain within a tight range and show less movement.

*Figure 2.2: Transition between range contraction and expansion in market.* (Chart shows initial flat zigzag labelled "Directionless Volatility" on the left, then rising trendline labelled "Directional Volatility" on the right.)

During range contraction, a stock's price typically trades within a narrow range. This indicates a period of consolidation, where buying and selling pressures are relatively balanced. This phase often occurs after a significant price movement, allowing the market to take a breather and traders to assess the next potential direction. As the price continues to trade within this confined range, tension builds up due to the accumulation of orders and the increasing anticipation among market participants. Eventually, this tension reaches an extreme point, causing a breakout and transitioning the stock into a phase of range expansion. During range expansion, the price moves more aggressively as new trends develop, fuelled by increased volatility.

In swing trading, volatility refers to the extent of price fluctuations in a stock. High volatility indicates significant

---

However, there are many stocks in the market that exhibit non-directional volatility, resulting in large moves on both sides. This is certainly not desirable for swing traders.

Parabolic moves are uncommon and usually happen in strong bull markets. They shoot up quickly, almost straight up, without waiting for sideways movement.

## Why Do Bases Form?

In stock trading, understanding how bases form is crucial as it reveals the underlying dynamics of demand and supply.

---

## PAGE 74 — printed page 66

Stocks fluctuate because different market participants— retail traders, institutional investors, and long-term investors— have varying objectives. Institutional money, guided by perceived future value rather than current price, significantly influences stock trends by creating and shaping chart patterns.

During downtrends, institutional investors, seeing potential, accumulate shares from emotional retail traders. This process forms bases that transfer ownership and stabilize the price. In uptrends, short-term traders often sell to take profits, creating temporary supply.

Bases form to absorb this supply, allowing the stock to consolidate and prepare for further advances. For swing traders, identifying these bases is essential. They signify areas where supply has been absorbed, increasing the likelihood of a sustained move higher and providing optimal entry and exit points.

Understanding this process enables traders to make informed decisions and maximize their profit potential during clear uptrends. This strategic insight transforms basing patterns into powerful indicators of future stock performance.

## Volatility Contraction Pattern (VCP)

The **Volatility Contraction Pattern (VCP)** is a popular strategy in stock trading created by **Mark Minervini** and works well in strong bull markets. This pattern shows how the price and trading volume behave when a stock is forming a base. As

This strategy helps traders identify the right time to enter the market during uptrends. ## Big Base

There's a saying: **'The bigger the base, the higher in space.'**

This means that stocks that have been in large trading ranges (bases) for several months to years often experience significant multi-month moves when they finally break out of these bases. Any base with a duration of **more than three-four weeks** could be considered a big base.

A big base can form in two ways: either after a significant downtrend or after a significant uptrend. In both scenarios, a substantial prior move—whether up or down—is a common factor.

---

## PAGE 80 — printed page 72

In the first scenario, a big base forms after a downtrend when institutions see value in the stock, but selling pressure persists due to 'bag holders' (investors who bought at higher prices) selling on any bounce, creating a large supply at higher levels. This creates a balance between demand and supply within a broader range, resulting in the formation of a big base. Over time, the supply diminishes as institutions continue to absorb it, eventually leading to a breakout and the start of a momentum phase.

## Base on Base

A **base-on-base setup** is a combination of two bases. It occurs when a stock doesn't continue rising after breaking out of a primary base. Instead, it forms another base before moving up again.

If the stock moves up too quickly before all the stock is bought, it might need to form another base to deal with that extra supply. A base-on-base setup is a bullish sign, especially if the first base was strong. When you see a second base forming on top of the first one, it's even better—it's like having double the strength.

## Anticipation Buy: Pre-empting a Move

If you're someone who's looking to catch those swift three to five-day swing-type moves, you always want your entries

An anticipation entry requires you to position yourself in a stock **before** the actual breakout occurs. The reason why most traders prefer to wait for a breakout is that it gives them confirmation that the stock is ready to continue the prior move. ## Selling into Strength

**'Selling into strength'** means selling a stock while its price is increasing, ideally at a point where it's gaining momentum and heading in the direction you anticipated. Instead of waiting for the stock to go down before taking an exit, you take action while it's still rising.

## Selling into Extended Moves

Trading involves interpreting price and volume data to make informed decisions about market movements.

## Setting a Stop-Loss

As mentioned earlier, no trading system is perfect, and no one can predict the market correctly all the time. In reality, most trading systems will only be right about **40-50% of the time**. That's why using a stop-loss on every trade is crucial.

A stop-loss is a price level where you decide that the reason you entered the trade is no longer valid. It's like buying insurance for your car which you buy every year but don't think of it as a waste of money. You know that it's likely that nothing's going to wrong and most likely the insurance will expire worthless, but you still take it just in case.

Similarly in trading, you need to stop losses early to protect you from bigger losses. If there's an accident, the insurance company covers the costs you would otherwise have to pay. Similarly, a stop-loss is like paying a small fee to the market to protect yourself from potential losses. While you might still get stopped out sometimes, using a stop-loss shields you from big losses.

### Stop-Loss Is Designed to Save You When Surprises Are Expensive

Many traders freeze when a trade goes against them, allowing small losses to grow into big ones. But remember, you always have a choice. Accepting a small loss early on is often better than waiting for a bigger loss later.

This helps you decide where to put your stop-loss—not too far away that you lose too much money if things go bad, but not too close that you get stopped out too soon.

If you're trading a mini-base setup, don't set your stop-loss too wide, or you might lose out on potential profits. For example, a **stop-loss larger than 1 ATR could be too big** for this type of trade, although it might work for someone who holds positions for longer.

Remember, keeping your stop-loss strategy the same for all your trades makes things simpler, especially if you're new to trading.

Selecting appropriate stocks and to purchase or sell them timely—is that all it takes to make money in the market consistently? Not quite. Another critical factor to consider is **position sizing**.

Position sizing addresses the 'how much' aspect of your trade—specifically, how much of a particular stock you should buy while adhering to your risk management rules. ## Risk Per Trade

**Risk per trade** is the money you're okay with losing on one trade. Let's say you have **Rs 1,00,000** to trade, and you decide to risk **1% per trade**—it means you're willing to lose **Rs 1,000** on each trade. ## Open Risk

**Open risk** refers to the potential losses that traders face from their active positions. Let's say you risk **0.5% of your account on every trade** and if you have three open trades, in this case, your open risk will be **1.5%**. As you trail, some or all of your stop-losses to cost the open risk starts to lower.

Managing open risk is crucial to safeguard capital and maintain profitability. If not managed properly, it can lead to significant losses and even wipe out your trading account.

Ensure that your total risk on open positions does not exceed **4-5% of your total account value** at any given time. This means that if all your open positions were to hit their stop-losses, your maximum loss should be limited to **4-5%** of your entire account value.

## Open Gains

## Expectancy

Imagine you're in a maze, trying to find your way out. In trading, knowing the **expectancy** of your system is like having a compass—it helps you figure out if you're moving in the right direction and how fast you're progressing.

Expectancy helps you understand how profitable your strategy is, and how much you can expect to make from it in the future, based on your past trades. It calculates the average amount you can expect to make for every rupee you risk.

> **Expectancy = (Winning Percentage × Average Win) − (Losing Percentage × Average Loss)**

- **Winning percentage:** The percentage of trades that make a profit.
- **Average win:** The average amount you gain on winning trades.
- **Losing percentage:** The percentage of trades that result in a loss.
- **Average loss:** The average amount you lose on losing trades.

For example, if you win **60%** of the time, with an average gain of **Rs 1,000**, and lose **40%** of the time, with an average loss of **Rs 400**, your expectancy would be:

> **Expectancy = (0.60 × 1000) − (0.40 × 400) = 600 − 160 = Rs 440**

This means you can expect to make **Rs 440** over a series of trades if you stick to your current strategy.

A **positive expectancy** suggests your strategy is likely to make money over time, while a **negative one** indicates potential losses.

## 1. Fixed Exposure Sizing

Fixed exposure sizing is a straightforward method where traders assign the same amount of money to each trade, regardless of the circumstances. ## Situational Awareness

Many folks helped shape how we understand situational awareness in trading. **Pradeep Bonde**, who's a great trading coach and a trader himself, is one of those influential figures who really emphasized home its importance and made it click for a lot of traders.

In trading, SA is all about understanding the market conditions, your trades, and, most importantly, understanding yourself.
